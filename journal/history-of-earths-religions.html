The history of Earth's religions

Published Tuesday, November 24, 2026

For as long as humans have told stories, they have asked the same big questions: Where did we come from? Why do we die? What is right? Religion is humanity's oldest and most persistent attempt to answer them. Here is a short tour of how those answers grew, spread and changed.

1. Prehistoric beginnings

The earliest hints of religious thought are graves. Neanderthals and early modern humans buried their dead, sometimes with tools, ochre or possibly flowers, which suggests a belief that death was not simply an ending. Cave paintings such as those at Lascaux (around 17,000 years ago) may have had ritual meaning. Göbekli Tepe in modern Turkey, built around 9500 BCE, is often called the world's oldest known temple, and it predates farming in the region.

Scholars generally describe early belief systems as animist: the idea that animals, places and natural forces have spirits. Shamans acted as go-betweens with that spirit world.

2. Gods of the first cities

When farming led to cities, religion became organized. Mesopotamia (from around 3500 BCE) had city gods, priests and ziggurat temples. Egypt developed a rich belief in the afterlife, with pharaohs treated as divine. These polytheistic systems had many gods, each with a role: sky, harvest, war, the dead.

3. The Axial Age (roughly 800 to 200 BCE)

Philosopher Karl Jaspers named this period the Axial Age, because so many lasting traditions took shape at once:

  • Hinduism grew from the Vedic traditions of India, with roots going back to around 1500 BCE. The Upanishads explored ideas like karma, rebirth and Brahman, the ultimate reality.
  • Buddhism began with Siddhartha Gautama (around the 5th century BCE), who taught a path to end suffering through the Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path.
  • Jainism, shaped by Mahavira in the same era, stressed non-violence toward all living things.
  • Judaism developed its monotheism, the belief in one God, through the Hebrew Bible and the prophets.
  • Zoroastrianism in Persia framed the world as a struggle between good and evil.
  • In China, Confucianism focused on ethics and social harmony, while Daoism taught living in balance with the Dao, the way of nature.

4. The great spreading religions

  • Christianity emerged in the 1st century CE from the teachings of Jesus of Nazareth. After the Roman emperor Constantine embraced it in the 4th century, it spread across the Roman world and later around the globe.
  • Islam began in the 7th century CE with the Prophet Muhammad in Arabia. Within about a century it reached from Spain to Central Asia.
  • Buddhism travelled the Silk Road to China, Korea, Japan and Southeast Asia.
  • Sikhism was founded by Guru Nanak in Punjab in the 15th century, teaching devotion to one God and equality of all people.

5. Splits, reforms and the modern world

Big religions rarely stay united. Christianity divided into Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic churches in 1054, and the Protestant Reformation began in 1517. Islam split early into Sunni and Shia branches. Buddhism developed Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana schools.

In the last few centuries, new movements appeared, such as the Bahá'í Faith in the 19th century, alongside the growth of secularism. Today, Pew Research Center estimates that Christians and Muslims together make up more than half of the world's population, while a fast-growing share of people describe themselves as religiously unaffiliated.

Why it matters

Religion has shaped laws, art, wars, calendars, music and how billions of people understand their lives. Knowing its history helps explain much of today's world, from holidays to politics.
