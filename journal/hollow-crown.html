The Paper Table · Post #1 · November 25, 2026

The Hollow Crown

PEN · PAPER · CARDS · DICE

The king is dead without an heir. Four houses rise. Only one will wear what remains.

That is the whole game, and it is the whole point of this series. The Paper Table is about games you can build from what's already in your house — a pen, some paper, a deck of cards, a pair of dice. No box to buy, no app to install, no pieces to lose under the couch. The Hollow Crown is the first of them, and it is the flagship: a long-form strategy game for 1–4 players, twelve provinces, twenty rounds, and a throne that nobody is going to hand you.

You will draw your kingdom out of a deck of cards, so no two campaigns are ever the same. You will spend four action points a round and never have enough. You will hold a King in your hand and spend ten minutes deciding whether it is an army or a battle. And at the end of it, win or lose, your map will tell the story of a war — the provinces you took, the ones you lost, the one you built a market in during round 3 and somehow still held in round 18.

The full rules are below, and the playable game page is linked at the bottom. Print it, play it, and tell me which house you are.

✎ Pen▭ Paper🂠 One deck of cards⚄ Two dice (2d6)◷ 60–120 min♟ 1–4 players

1. A round, played out

Before the rules, a round — so you can feel the game before you read it. This is solo play, Normal difficulty, Round 3. You are the Hart. You hold P5, P6 and P9. The Realm holds P3, P4 and P8 across the map. You start the round with 11 Gold, 7 Army, 2 Influence, and two cards in hand: the K♥ and the 4♠. Tap through the round.

2. Setup

Draw a 3×4 grid on a sheet of paper — twelve boxes, P1 through P12 in reading order. Only orthogonal neighbours are adjacent (no diagonals). Each box is a province, with three values: Value (1–5, how much gold it produces), Defense (1–4, how hard it is to take), and Owner.

Step 1 — Draw the provinces

Shuffle the deck. Draw 12 cards, one per province in order P1→P12. Each card sets that province's Value and Defense:

Card drawnValueDefenseFlavour
Rank 2–511Poor farmland
Rank 6–821Common province
Rank 9–1031Wealthy province
Jack or Queen32Fortified town
King43Great city
Ace54The old capital

Write each province's Value and Defense on the map. Leave the Owner blank for now.

Step 2 — Draw your House

Each player draws one card from the deck and looks at it privately. Its suit is your House for the whole campaign (see §3). Return the cards to the deck and shuffle thoroughly.

Step 3 — Choose starting provinces

Roll 2d6 for turn order (highest first). In turn order, each player claims one unclaimed province of their choice. Then repeat in reverse turn order for a second province, so the last player to pick first also picks first the second time. Every player starts with two provinces.

Step 4 — Starting resources

Gold8
Army3 (split between your two provinces; write the garrison in each)
Influence0
Cards in hand2

Step 5 — The Realm Deck

Shuffle the remaining cards. This is the Realm Deck — it drives events and replenishes hands. Discarded cards form a face-up discard pile beside it. When the Realm Deck runs out, shuffle the discard pile to form a new Realm Deck. You will need this rule. In testing, the deck ran out in every single long game.

A note on the map

Because province values are drawn randomly, no two campaigns are the same. A game where the Ace lands in the centre is a very different game from one where it lands on the edge. Read the map before you pick your starting provinces — that is your first strategic decision.

3. The four houses

Your House ability is set at setup and lasts the whole campaign. It is your permanent edge — play to it. Tap a house.

In a 4-player game, each House will likely appear once. In a 2- or 3-player game, some Houses will be absent and others may repeat — that is fine. If two players draw the same suit at setup, they share the ability; no redraw is needed.

4. The round

The game runs for 20 rounds. Each round has four phases, resolved in order.

I. The Realm Stirs

Draw the top card of the Realm Deck and resolve it as an Event (see §7). Events affect all players. The card is then discarded.

II. Dawn

Each player, simultaneously:

  • Draws 1 card from the Realm Deck (2 if your House is the Raven).
  • Gains Gold equal to the total Value of provinces they own, plus 2 per Market they control.
  • Gains Influence equal to the number of Temples they control.

III. Command

Each player, in turn order, has 4 Action Points (AP) to spend on actions (see §5). You may take the same action more than once unless stated otherwise. You may also pass at any time; unspent AP is lost.

IV. Winter

Each player, simultaneously:

  • Pays upkeep: 1 Gold per 3 Army (rounded up). If you cannot pay, lose 2 Army per unpaid Gold.
  • Discards down to 5 cards if holding more.

Then check for victory (see §9). If no one has won, begin the next round.

5. Actions

On your Command phase, spend AP on any of the following. Actions may be taken in any order and repeated unless noted.

ActionCostEffect
Levy1 AP + 1 GoldGain 2 Army. Place them in any province you own.
Diplomacy1 APGain 1 Influence.
Play a Card1 APPlay a card from your hand for its suit action (see §7). The card is discarded.
Claim2 APTake control of an adjacent neutral province. You must move at least 1 Army into it from an adjacent province you own. The province becomes yours; write your name in it.
Attack2 AP + 1 CardAttack an adjacent province owned by another player. See §6 Combat. The card is spent whether the attack succeeds or not.
Build2 AP + 3 GoldBuild one improvement in a province you own (see §8). One of each type per province.

Free actions

Once per round, during your Command phase, you may redistribute armies between any adjacent provinces you own. This costs no AP. Armies may move through a chain of your provinces in a single turn, but each province may only send or receive once per round.

The 4 AP budget

Four AP is deliberately tight. You can make two attacks, or claim one province and levy twice, or build one improvement and still act once more. Every round forces you to choose between expanding, fighting, and investing — and you will never have enough AP to do all three. That tension is the game.

6. Combat

When you take the Attack action, choose a province you own with at least 1 Army, and an adjacent province owned by another player. Commit any number of armies from your province (they must all come from that one province). Then resolve:

The rolls

  • Attacker rolls 2d6 and adds the number of armies committed, plus the rank of any one card discarded for a combat bonus.
  • Defender rolls 2d6 and adds the province's garrison plus twice its Defense value.

The result

OutcomeEffect
Attacker wins (higher total)Defender loses armies equal to the difference (capped at their garrison — if the difference exceeds the garrison, all defenders die and the province falls). Attacker loses armies equal to half the difference, rounded up. The attacker captures the province and places any surviving committed armies in it as the new garrison. If the defender has surviving armies, they retreat to an adjacent friendly province (or are destroyed if none exists).
Defender wins (higher or tied total)Attacker loses armies equal to the difference (capped at committed armies). Defender loses armies equal to half the difference, rounded up. Any surviving attacking armies return to their origin province. The province is unchanged.

Losses are capped: attacker losses are capped at the number of armies committed — you cannot lose more armies than you sent.

Card bonus

Before rolling, you may discard one card from your hand to add its rank to your attack roll. This is separate from the card spent to take the Attack action — you may spend one card for the action and another for the bonus, or use a single card for both. The card used for the Attack action is always spent; the card used for the bonus is also spent.

A worked example

Attacker has 6 Army in P4 and attacks P3, held by an enemy with 3 garrison and Defense 2. The attacker discards a 7♣ for +7 and rolls 8. Total: 8 + 6 + 7 = 21.

Defender rolls 7. Total: 7 + 3 + (2 × 2) = 14.

Attacker wins by 7. Defender loses 3 garrison (all dead — the difference exceeds the garrison). Attacker loses 4 Army (half of 7, rounded up), leaving 2. The attacker captures P3 with a garrison of 2. The defender's province is lost.

7. Cards — three uses, one decision

Cards in hand serve three purposes. Every card is useful — the question is always which use is best right now.

Suit actions (1 AP)

SuitActionEffect
♠MusterGain 3 Army in any province you own.
♥RallyGain 3 Gold.
♦TradeGain 2 Influence.
♣WhisperDraw 2 cards.

Combat bonus (free, during Attack)

Discard a card to add its rank to your attack roll. A King adds +13 — enough to swing almost any battle — but you lose the card and its suit action forever.

Held in reserve

Cards in hand are not vulnerable to events and can be spent at the perfect moment. Holding the right card for the right round is often stronger than spending it early.

The tension of a high card

A King of Spades can be a Muster for +3 Army or a +13 combat bonus. Using it for the Muster is safe but small; saving it for combat is explosive but situational. Every high card in your hand is a decision you have not yet made.

8. Events — the Realm stirs

At the start of every round, draw one card and read the event matching its suit and rank. Events affect all players unless stated otherwise. Tap a suit.

9. Buildings

Buildings are permanent investments. Each costs 3 Gold and 2 AP to build, and each province may hold one of each type.

BuildingEffect
Market+2 Gold income each Dawn from this province.
Temple+1 Influence income each Dawn from this province.
Walls+1 Defense to this province (making it harder to attack).

Write buildings in the province box on your map, abbreviated: M, T, W. A province with all three is marked M T W and is a serious stronghold.

The investment decision

3 Gold and 2 AP is a significant cost. A Market pays for itself in two rounds and is pure profit thereafter — but only if you hold the province that long. A Temple builds Influence slowly, which matters only in the final scoring and in the Coronation event. Walls pay for themselves the first time someone attacks and fails.

10. Victory & scoring

Immediate victory

If at any point — including mid-round — a player controls 7 or more of the 12 provinces, they win immediately. The game ends at once.

Legacy victory

If no one has won by the end of Round 20, calculate each player's Legacy Score:

ComponentPoints
Each province controlled× 5
Each point of Influence× 2
Each building (Market, Temple, or Walls)× 3
Gold on hand÷ 3 (rounded down)
Army on the map÷ 2 (rounded down)

The highest Legacy Score wins. Ties are broken by: most provinces → most Influence → most Gold → most Army → roll 2d6.

The Exile

If you control no provinces at Dawn, you may place 2 Army in any unclaimed neutral province as a new capital. If no neutral province remains, you are eliminated: remove your pieces from the map and score nothing. Nobody is ever out of the game on a bad round — but the second bad round can end you.

What the scoring rewards

Provinces are worth the most, but Influence and buildings are close behind. A player who spends the whole game fighting may hold six provinces and still lose to a player who holds four, built three Markets, and accumulated ten Influence. The scoring is deliberately designed to make pure aggression a losing strategy.

11. Solo mode — face the Realm

In solo play, you face The Realm — an automated opponent driven by the deck. The Realm does not track gold or cards; it simply acts.

Solo setup

  • Draw the map as normal.
  • You choose 3 starting provinces (in any connected group).
  • The Realm controls 3 provinces on the opposite side of the map, each with a garrison of 2.
  • You start with 8 Gold, 3 Army, 0 Influence, 2 cards.
  • The Realm has no gold, no hand, and no buildings.

The solo round

  1. The Realm Stirs — draw and resolve an event as normal.
  2. Dawn — you gain income and draw as normal.
  3. Command — you take your 4 AP of actions.
  4. The Realm Acts — draw Realm cards and resolve the Realm's actions (see below): one card on Easy, two cards on Normal and Hard, resolved one after the other.
  5. Winter — pay upkeep and check victory as normal.

The Realm's action

Card drawnRealm action
♠Attack. The Realm attacks your weakest border province (the one with the lowest garrison). It rolls 2d6 + 4. Your defense rolls 2d6 + garrison + (Defense × 2). Resolve combat normally. If the Realm wins, it captures the province and places 2 garrison in it.
♥Reinforce. The Realm adds 2 Army to its most-fortified province.
♦Expand. The Realm claims an adjacent neutral province (if any), placing 1 garrison in it.
♣Conspire. The Realm draws a card and sets it aside face-down. The next time the Realm attacks, it adds +3 to its roll. (Only one such bonus can be active at a time.)

Solo victory

You win by controlling 8 or more provinces at any point, or by having the higher Legacy Score at the end of Round 20. The Realm wins by controlling 8 or more provinces, or by having the higher Legacy Score. The Realm's Legacy Score is its provinces × 5 plus its Army ÷ 2 (rounded down). It has no Gold, Influence, or buildings.

Difficulty

ModeRealm setupRealm behaviour
Easy3 provinces, 1 garrison eachAttacks roll 2d6 + 3; acts once per round
Normal3 provinces, 2 garrison eachAttacks roll 2d6 + 4; draws two Realm cards per round and acts twice
Hard4 provinces, 3 garrison eachAttacks roll 2d6 + 5; draws two Realm cards per round and acts twice
Playing solo well

The Realm is relentless but predictable — it always attacks your weakest border. That means a single strong garrison on the threatened side can hold off several attacks while you expand elsewhere. Solo play rewards defensive positioning and patience far more than multiplayer does.

12. Variants

Once you have played the game straight, these are the ways to bend it. Two of them are new in this version.

VariantRule
The Long ReignPlay 30 rounds instead of 20. All players will reach their 7-province victory threshold eventually — the game becomes a race to 7, not a consolidation. Recommended for experienced players who want a longer, more brutal game.
The Short WarPlay 12 rounds instead of 20. Victory threshold drops to 6 provinces. Faster, sharper, more aggressive. Good for a single evening's play.
Fog of WarPlayers do not reveal their gold, influence, or card hands to one another until final scoring. Provinces and armies remain public. This creates uncertainty and rewards bluffing.
DiplomacyPlayers may make binding agreements — "I will not attack you for 3 rounds if you give me 2 Gold now." Broken agreements carry no in-game penalty, but the table remembers. Best with 3–4 players.
Written TreatiesDuring your Command phase, you and another player may write and sign a treaty on paper ("no attacks between us for 3 rounds", "2 Gold now for safe passage"). A treaty is binding. Breaking it costs the breaker 3 Influence immediately, paid the moment the breaking action is taken. A treaty may not force a player to attack a third party. It ends when its term expires or when it is broken.
Asymmetric MapInstead of the standard 3×4 grid, draw a map of 12 provinces in any shape — a long line, a ring, two islands connected by a single bridge. The shape of the map becomes a strategic constraint. Agree on the shape before setup.
The UsurperOne player is secretly the Usurper. They win by holding the Ace province (the old capital) at the end of Round 20, regardless of Legacy Score. Roles are drawn secretly at setup and revealed only at the end of Round 20. Best with 4 players.
Ironman SoloIn solo mode, if the Realm ever controls 8+ provinces, you lose immediately — no comeback. Play Hard mode. This is the "true" solo challenge.

13. How to play well

A few principles that separate winning play from losing play:

Expand early, fight late

Neutral provinces are free provinces. In the first four rounds, every AP spent claiming is worth more than an AP spent attacking. The player who grabs the neutral map first enters the mid-game with an income advantage that compounds.

Build on your safest province

A Market on a border province is a gift to whoever attacks you next. A Market on a province surrounded by your own territory is a permanent engine. Place buildings behind your lines.

Cards are ammunition, not treasure

A hand of five cards is five potential combat bonuses or five Muster actions. Holding them "for later" is usually a mistake unless you are specifically waiting to defend a province. Spend aggressively in the mid-game; conserve only when a critical battle is coming.

Watch the event deck

If the last three events have been ♥ Fortune, the deck is due for ♠ Conflict. A player who anticipates a bad event round — by not overextending — will survive what kills their rivals.

Influence is quiet but heavy

Influence is worth 2 points each at the end. A player who takes Diplomacy every round accumulates 20+ Influence by game's end — 40 Legacy points, more than most players' province scores. Do not neglect it.

Know when to stop

The 7-province victory is the fastest path to a win, but it also paints a target on your back. Sometimes the winning move is to sit at 5 provinces, build Markets, and let your rivals exhaust each other before you strike.

14. Played 1,000 times by a computer

Before this post went up, the game was played a thousand times by a computer — a simple bot that claims free provinces, funnels its armies to its weakest border, and attacks only when the numbers favour it. Here is what it found, and what changed because of it.

The solo game was too easy. On the original rules, the bot won all 1,000 solo games on Normal, in about four and a half rounds. The Realm only acted once per round, and the bot simply walked across the neutral provinces while it waited. So the Realm was given a second action: on Normal, it now draws two Realm cards and acts twice per round. After the change, the bot still won 96.3% of 1,000 games — but it took about six rounds, and the Realm took 3.7% of games outright. The game is still winnable; it is no longer a walk.

The reshuffle rule is not optional. In the twenty-round Legacy variant, the Realm Deck ran out in 1,000 out of 1,000 games — an average of one and a half reshuffles per campaign. The rule that was added to the game ("when the Realm Deck runs out, shuffle the discard pile into a new deck") is not a corner case. It is load-bearing.

The houses are closer than they look. Over a thousand full campaigns, average Legacy Scores:

🐍 Serpent375
🦌 Hart349
🐦‍⬛ Raven335
🐺 Wolf335

The Serpent's extra Influence every Dawn adds up over twenty rounds, and the Wolf's +1 to attack rolls is the weakest edge in a game where most battles are won by position, not by a point. The Raven's edge is subtler — it churns through the deck faster (two reshuffles a game against the Wolf's one and a half), which means more cards in hand, more options, and more ways to be in the right place. If the Wolf feels flat at your table, give it +1 Defense as well and see what happens.

One honest caveat: the bot plays near-perfectly. It never gets attached to a province, never holds a King for sentimental reasons, never attacks out of spite. A human will find Normal harder than the bot's 96% suggests — and Hard, where the Realm starts with four provinces and attacks at +5, is the real challenge. If you beat Hard, tell me.
