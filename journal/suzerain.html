Suzerain: The Fifty-Two Crowns

PEN · PAPER · CARDS

You are an upstart warlord in a broken land. Four great powers hold the continent between them, and the deck in your hands is both your army and your enemy.

That is Suzerain. It is the second game in The Paper Table — games you can build from what's already in your house — and it is the loneliest one: a solo campaign of world conquest, played with one deck of cards and a pen. Twenty provinces drawn from the deck. Four rival suits waiting to take them back. Fourteen provinces to win, and a clock called the Reckoning that starts ticking the moment the deck runs out.

Every card you draw is a card your enemies cannot draw. Every card you hoard is a weapon withheld from fate. And every card you spend is a card that will never come back to be reshuffled — so the deck gets thinner, the nights get meaner, and the question the game keeps asking is the same one every warlord faces: how fast can you win, and what will it cost you to get there?

The full rules are below, and the playable game page is linked at the bottom. Print it, play it slow, and name your provinces.

✎ Pen▭ Paper🂠 One deck of cards◷ 60–120 min♟ 1 player

1. A season, played out

Before the rules, a season — so you can feel the game before you read it. This is Season One, Threat 1, straight from the game's own sample season. You hold 5 cards, 4 Gold, 6 Troops, and 5 provinces. The Sun Court sits two provinces from your border. Tap through the season.

2. Setup

Draw a 5×4 grid on a sheet of paper — twenty boxes in reading order. Only orthogonal neighbours are adjacent (no diagonals). Each box is a province, with three values: Owner, Defense, and Gold value.

Step 1 — Build the world

Shuffle the deck. Draw 20 cards, one per province in order. Each card creates one province:

Setup cardProvince ownerDefenseGold value
Rank 2–6Neutral (independent)31
Rank 7–10The card's suit42
Jack · Queen · KingThe card's suit52
AceThe card's suit63

On a 2–6, the suit is ignored — the province is independent. Draw an 8♦ and you have a Merchant League province, Defense 4, worth 2 Gold. Draw a 4♠ and you have a Neutral province, Defense 3, worth 1 Gold.

Name each province as you place it. Names make the map feel real and make your log readable.

Step 2 — Choose your home

Pick any Neutral province. That is your capital. Record in the ledger: Gold 2, Troops 3, Hand 0, Threat 1.

Step 3 — Deal fate

Shuffle the remaining 32 cards. Set aside 12 of them face-down — these are the Sealed Fates, and they take no part in this campaign. The other 20 cards are the Fate Deck. It is your resource and your enemy.

Card values: Ace = 1 or 11 (choose when played). 2–10 = face value. Jack, Queen, King = 10.

Two sheets of paper

You need a map sheet (the 5×4 grid, with each province's name, owner initial, Defense, and Gold value) and a ledger sheet (Gold, Troops, hand, Threat, rival province counts, titles). Cards in hand stay face-up in front of you — but write them down too, so the game can be rebuilt exactly if they're disturbed. Use pencil. This map will be redrawn many times.

3. The turn — one Season

A campaign is a series of Seasons. Each Season has three phases, resolved in order.

I. Dawn

  1. Collect Gold: gain Gold equal to the total value of provinces you own.
  2. Draw to hand: draw until your hand reaches hand size (start: 5). If the Fate Deck runs out mid-draw, see §6, The Reckoning.

II. Day — take up to 3 actions

Each action costs 1 card from hand unless noted. You may take the same action more than once, but no more than 3 actions in a Day. Spending Gold (below) is free and does not count toward the 3.

The spent pile

Cards you play, spend, or discard go to a spent pile and leave the game — they are never reshuffled. Only cards flipped at Night go to the discard pile, which is what the Reckoning reshuffles. Every card you spend is a card that never reaches the discard pile. That is the whole economy of the game.

ActionCostEffect
Conquer1+ cardsAttack an adjacent province. See §4 Combat.
Conscript1 cardGain Troops equal to half the card's value, rounded down, min 1 (face cards = 5).
Fortify1 card+1 permanent Defense to one province you own (max 8).
Settle1 card+1 Gold value to one province you own (max 3).
Treaty1 cardName a rival. The next time that rival would activate at Night, it does not — whenever that happens. The card must match the rival's suit.
Rally2 cardsDiscard both, draw 2. Hand filtering.

Spend Gold at any time during Day:

  • 1 Gold → 1 Troop (max 3 per Season)
  • 2 Gold → draw 1 card
  • 3 Gold → +1 permanent Defense to a province you own

III. Night — the world moves

Flip cards from the Fate Deck equal to your current Threat (starts at 1). Resolve each flip separately:

  1. The suit tells you which rival acts. Ignore the rank for choosing.
  2. Find that rival's target: the province nearest to any territory they own that they do not own. Use shortest path across the grid. If two provinces are equally near, the rival targets the one with the lowest Defense; if still tied, you choose.
  3. Resolve the attack using the flipped card's value plus your current Threat as their force (A = 11 — so a Queen at Threat 2 attacks with force 12):
    TargetResult
    NeutralThey take it if force ≥ its Defense. Otherwise nothing happens.
    YoursYou may defend by playing cards from hand and/or spending Troops (1 Troop = +2 Defense), adding to the province's Defense. If your total ≥ their force, the attack is repelled and you keep the province. If not, the province falls to that rival — remove your marker.
    Another rival'sThey take it if force ≥ Defense + 2.
  4. If a rival owns no provinces at all, the flip becomes a Raid: you lose 1 Troop and 1 Gold. If you have no Troops and no Gold, the nearest province you own loses 1 Defense permanently.

Cards flipped at Night go to a discard pile, face up. They are not available to you.

4. Combat — Conquer

Choose an adjacent province you own to launch the attack from, and an adjacent province you don't own as the target. Play cards from hand. Your total must equal or exceed the target's Defense. Add +2 to its Defense if the province belongs to its own suit — patriots resist. Neutral provinces get no bonus.

You may also spend Troops during an attack: 1 Troop = +2 to your total, declared before comparing. Troops spent for an attack boost are lost.

Your total vs. DefenseOutcome
Total ≥ Defense + 5Province taken. No losses.
Total ≥ Defense + 1Province taken. Lose 1 Troop.
Total = Defense exactlyProvince taken. Lose 2 Troops (pyrrhic).
Total < DefenseAttack fails. Lose 2 Troops and all cards played.

Captured provinces keep their Defense and Gold value. Enemy provinces you take are now simply yours — no bonus, no penalty. If an outcome would make you lose more Troops than you have, you lose what you have — Troops never go below 0.

Troops are a single army recorded as one number in your ledger — there are no per-province garrisons. That is why defending a province at Night costs cards and Troops from your one pool, and why every Troop you spend on an attack is a Troop you cannot spend on a defense.

Champions

When you conquer a province by playing a King as part of your attack total, name a Champion in your ledger. A Champion grants +2 to any Conquer total launched from a province adjacent to the Champion's province. Each province can hold one Champion; if the province falls to a rival, the Champion is lost.

The cost of a cheap win

A province taken by exactly its Defense costs you 2 Troops. A province taken by 5 over costs you nothing. The difference between a careful conquest and a desperate one is measured in the army you have left for Night — and Night comes every single Season.

5. The four rivals

Name them yourself and write the names in your ledger. The suits are the engine; the flavor is yours. Mechanically, all four rivals act the same way at Night — the flipped card's suit picks which one moves, and its force is the card's value plus your Threat. What differs is what they mean, and which of them is sitting on your border. Tap a suit.

A Treaty (see §3) is your one way to silence a rival: play a card matching its suit, and the next time that rival would activate at Night, it does not — whenever that happens. The card is banked until the rival's next activation actually occurs.

6. The Reckoning

When the Fate Deck is exhausted:

  1. Shuffle the discard pile into a new Fate Deck.
  2. Threat increases by 1 (maximum 4). More cards flip at Night.
  3. If you reshuffle while Threat is already 4, the campaign ends — score your holdings.

If the discard pile is empty when the Fate Deck runs out, the campaign ends — score your holdings.

Entrenchment — At each Reckoning, before the new Fate Deck is shuffled, each rival adds +1 Defense to one of its border provinces (the one nearest your territory; maximum 8). The world does not just get meaner as the clock runs. It gets harder to take back.

This is the clock

Every card you spend is a card that never reaches the discard pile to be reshuffled. Hoarding cards slows the Reckoning but starves your expansion. That tension is the whole game. The Sealed Fates make it bite sooner: twelve cards never enter the campaign at all, so the twenty-card Fate Deck runs out fast, and the first Reckoning arrives while you are still small.

7. Winning & losing

VictoryControl 14 of 20 provinces, or reduce all four rivals to zero territory.
DefeatLose your last province, or any single rival controls 10+ provinces.
Endgame scoreIf the Reckoning ends the campaign: 1 point per province, 2 per fortified province, 1 per 5 Gold, 1 per 3 Troops. Record it as your dynasty's legacy.

Titles

At these milestones, claim a permanent boon and write it in the ledger.

Provinces heldTitleBoon

The Suzerain title used to be unreachable — twelve provinces was already a win. With the win moved to fourteen, the title is now a real mid-game milestone: two provinces from victory, and an extra card every Dawn to carry you the rest of the way.

8. Variants

Once you have played the game straight, these are the ways to bend it.

VariantRule
Hard modeThreat starts at 2.
Slow burnRivals only act on a flip if the card's rank is 6 or higher. Longer, more builder-friendly campaign.
Rival warWhen two rivals fight, the loser's card is set aside. The rival with the most set-aside cards is the Ascendant and flips twice at Night.
Dynasty modeWhen a campaign ends, note your final province count. Next campaign, start with Troops equal to half that number (max 6). Your previous empire's ruins enrich the new world.

9. How to play well

A few principles that separate a dynasty from a footnote:

Spend, don't hoard

A card in your hand is a card that is not in the discard pile, and only the discard pile comes back at a Reckoning. Hoarding five cards to feel safe makes the clock run faster, not slower. Spend the card. Take the province.

Defend what you just took

A new province is the rival's nearest target — Night will come for it before it has paid for itself. Keep a card and a Troop in reserve after every conquest, or the province you won in the Day will be gone by the Night.

Treaties are cheap insurance

One card matching a rival's suit buys you a skipped activation whenever it would have happened. If the Sun Court is two provinces from your border and you are about to expand toward it, a Treaty with ♥ costs less than the province it saves.

Fortify the border, not the capital

Your capital is rarely the province Night attacks — the nearest province to a rival's territory is. Spend your 3 Gold on the province the rivals can actually reach.

Watch the deck, not just the map

Count the Fate Deck. When it is nearly empty, a Reckoning is coming: Threat rises, rivals entrench, and Night flips one more card. A Season spent consolidating before the deck runs out is worth more than a province taken the Season after.

Fourteen is a long way from twelve

The last two provinces are the hardest. By then the rivals are entrenched, the deck is thin, and Threat is at 2 or 3. The Sovereign title's +1 Defense to everything you own — including what you conquer next — is what carries you through the endgame. Get to nine provinces on purpose.

10. Played 1,000 times by a computer

Before this post went up, the game was played a thousand times by a computer — a simple bot that conquers, conscripts, fortifies, and defends when the numbers favour it. Here is what it found, and what changed because of it.

The first version was so easy the clock never ran. On the original rules, the bot won all 1,000 campaigns, in about 2.3 seasons — and the Reckoning ended none of them. Day had no action limit, so the bot simply repeated actions freely and took the map before the deck had time to thin. The game's whole premise — a clock that punishes you for spending — never got to speak.

So the game was rebuilt around the clock. Five changes, each aimed at the same problem:

ChangeWhat it does
Day capped at 3 actionsYou can no longer chain a whole campaign in one Season. Three actions, then Night comes.
Every province +1 DefenseConquests cost more cards — Neutral 3, suit provinces 4, face cards 5, Aces 6.
Win moved to 14 provincesTwo more conquests before the game ends, and the Suzerain title at 12 is now reachable mid-game.
Sealed FatesTwelve cards are set aside at setup and never enter the campaign. The Fate Deck is 20 cards, so it runs out fast.
Night with teethA rival's force is now its card's value plus your Threat — so the attacks get stronger as the clock runs.

After all five changes, the bot still won 97.9% of 1,000 campaigns — but the game underneath it is different now: campaigns last about 5.0 seasons instead of 2.3, the Fate Deck reshuffles about once per campaign instead of never, and the Reckoning ends 2.1% of campaigns outright. The clock is real. It just isn't fast enough to catch a player who never wastes a card.

Before tuningAfter tuning
Bot win rate (1,000 campaigns)100%97.9%

One honest caveat: the bot plays near-perfectly. It never gets attached to a province, never holds a King for sentimental reasons, never spends a Troop on a conquest it could have won without one. A human will bleed cards to Night, waste a Season on a bad draw, and watch the Reckoning arrive while still holding five provinces. For a person, this game is tenser than 97.9% suggests. If you beat it on Hard mode — Threat starts at 2 — tell me.
