The Paper Cabinet

PEN · PAPER · CARDS

Seven solitaire games in one box — one deck, one pen, seven different ways to lose an evening.

The first three posts in this series were single games, each with its own world: a kingdom to conquer, a crown to win, a fortune to read. The Cabinet is different. It is the drawer you open when you are alone, it is late, and you want a game that fits in the space between the lamp and the coffee cup. Seven of them, actually — a lighthouse, an auction house, a workshop, a throne room, a lying map, a debt collector, and a churchyard. Every one of them is played alone, with the same deck and the same pen. Pick one, play it to the end, and only then open the next drawer.

That is the other thing about the Cabinet: it is a box, not a book. The games do not build on each other. They are seven separate answers to the same question — how much game can you get out of fifty-two cards and a sheet of paper? The answers range from twenty minutes to over an hour, from a quiet puzzle about burying the dead to a thirteen-night siege against your own nerves. One of them was unwinnable when it was written. One of them could not be lost. Both of those are fixed now, and the story of how is at the bottom of this post.

The full rules for all seven live on one page, linked below. This post is the tour — one tight section per game, the hook, the one rule that makes it sing, and what the computer thought of it.

1. The Lantern Keepers

Survival horror · 45–75 min · Track Oil, Wick, Nerve, Night

The hook: you are the keeper of a lighthouse on a bare rock, and relief is thirteen nights away.

What you're doing: every night you draw three event cards — storms that drink your Oil, visitors you must choose to shelter or turn away, supply boats, omens that chew at your Nerve — and then you pay the lamp. Oil 16, Wick 4, Nerve 10, Night 1: that is everything you own, written in four numbers at the top of the page. Survive all thirteen nights with the lamp still burning and you win.

The rule that makes it sing

Wick starts at 4 and drops on nights 3, 6, 9, and 12 — so if you never repair it, the light dies on night 12, one night short. Repairing costs 3 Oil, and Oil is also what the lamp burns every single night, and a Supply card only brings 3. Every repair is a choice between the lamp's body and its fuel, and the game never lets you have both.

Sim note: a good player survives 35.0% of the time — ordinary play, 21.3%. The keeper who never repairs the wick loses every time, by construction.

2. The Auction of Small Gods

Bidding & economics · 30–45 min · Track Coins, lots won, rival purses

The hook: five rounds, twenty lots, three rivals with deeper pockets and stranger tastes than yours.

What you're doing: you have 40 Coins and a 5×4 grid to fill. Each lot is a card flipped face-up, and each rival bids by flipping a card of their own: the Widow bids high on red suits, the Colonel on black, the Child on face cards and aces. You are building sets of three or more of a suit, pairs of a rank, and — if you are lucky — exactly one 9, the collector's lucky card.

The rule that makes it sing

Ties go to you. And once per round, the Catalog lets you peek at the next lot before you bid. The whole game is the patient collector's game: you cannot win the lots the rivals want — the Widow will outbid you on hearts and the Colonel on spades — so you win the cheap lots they ignore, the off-suit cards that still complete your sets, and you let them burn their purses on each other. Their purses are 55, 55, and 45, and they are not bottomless.

Sim note: a patient player wins 34.6% of auctions — and wins by about 15 points when they win, loses by about 19 when they don't. The margin is the story.

3. The Machine

Optimization puzzle · 20–40 min · Track Flow, Target, slots, turns

The hook: you are an inventor with a workbench, a blueprint, and a deadline — build a machine that produces exactly what the patron ordered.

What you're doing: flip a card, multiply its rank by 6, and that is your Target. Your boiler starts at Flow 12. You have eight cards on the bench and eight turns to install four of them, left to right, into the machine. Gears add their rank. Springs multiply. Conduits subtract. Valves divide. Hit the Target exactly and it is a Masterwork, worth 3; within 3, a Success; within 8, a Partial. A campaign is three machines, scored out of 9.

The rule that makes it sing

Two of them. First, face cards are worth 11, 12, and 13 here — Jack 11, Queen 12, King 13, Ace 11 — the only game in the Cabinet that counts them that way, because the whole puzzle is arithmetic and the numbers have to be exact. Second, the Spare Part: once per campaign you may hold a fifth component in reserve and swap it into any one slot before resolution. It is the only undo in a game where installed components are locked forever, and knowing when to spend it is the whole skill.

Sim note: a campaign averages 5.79 out of 9 — respectable, the patron pays but grumbles — and with the Spare Part in play, nobody scores 0 or 1 anymore.

4. The Regent's Ledger

Political management · 40–60 min · Track four Loyalties, Treasury, Month, hand

The hook: you govern in the name of a child king, and the treasury is never quite enough.

What you're doing: twelve months, four factions — the Crown, the Church, the Guilds, the People — each starting at Loyalty 7. Every month brings two council issues, one per card drawn, and each one you must pay off, appease with a matching card, or ignore at the cost of Loyalty. Income is 5 Gold a month. Crises strike in months 4, 8, and 12. In month 6 the king makes a demand. In month 12 he comes of age.

The rule that makes it sing

The Crown is the one that can end you. Surviving twelve months is not enough — at Month 12 the Crown's Loyalty must stand at 6 or higher, or the king purges you regardless of how beloved you are elsewhere. You can spend the whole year keeping the Guilds happy and still lose on the last day, to the boy you were protecting. A Marriage — 8 Gold, once per game, Crown +2 and another faction +1 — is the closest thing to a safety net you get.

Sim note: 38.3% of regents survive the year. Two in three do not — and the Crown is usually the reason.

5. The Cartographer's Lie

Exploration & risk · 30–50 min · Track Provisions, position, a 6×6 map

The hook: you bought a map of an island from a man who smiled too much.

What you're doing: thirty-six cards face-down in a 6×6 grid, start at the bottom-left, treasure at the top-right, 20 Provisions in your pack. Six of the cards are false landmarks — spades, ranks 2 through 7 — and stepping on one costs 3 extra Provisions, because you have wasted time at a place that does not exist. The shortest path is 10 moves. You have 10 spare.

The rule that makes it sing

Probing costs the same as moving — 1 Provision either way. Knowing and going are the same price, and that is the entire negotiation of the game: every probe is a move you did not make. And when a wrong turn strands you, Retrace (2 Provisions) lets you step back onto a cell you have already visited — the rule that saves wandering players, because without it a bad turn can leave you with no legal move at all.

Sim note: 64.9% win rate — the friendliest game in the Cabinet, and the only one that needed no tuning at all.

6. The Debt

Push-your-luck · 30–45 min · Track Gold, Health, Day

The hook: you owe a man named Corvo eighty gold, and Corvo does not extend credit.

What you're doing: ten days. Every day you choose one thing: Work for a safe 7 Gold. Gamble — draw three cards, red beats black and you gain 20, black wins and you lose 12. Hustle — draw two cards and take their combined ranks in Gold, but any black card draws a second collector that evening. Or Hide, gain nothing, and skip the collector entirely. And every evening that you did not hide, a card is drawn: black suit means a collector found you, and you lose Health.

The rule that makes it sing

Honest work for ten days is 70 gold. Ten short. The arithmetic is the trap: you must gamble or hustle, and both cost you Health, and Health is the only thing standing between you and the collectors. The real question each morning is not how to make money — it is how much of yourself you are willing to spend to make it faster. There is also a Friend in Low Places, once per game, who makes one collector hit disappear.

Sim note: 14.2% win rate. The hardest game in the Cabinet that can still be won — and the computer's winners finished with less than 2 Health left on average.

7. The Gravekeeper

Quiet horror · 20–30 min · Track Rest, Dread, Day

The hook: the dead arrive one by one, and each must be buried where they belong.

What you're doing: nine days, one card a day, placed in a 3×3 churchyard. A card buried in a row whose patron suit matches its own is at rest. A card buried in the wrong row is restless: +1 Dread, immediately — +2 if it is a face card. A face card buried right gives +1 Rest instead, and the village is grateful. Three rites, once a day: the Stone moves a card, the Song lets you see tomorrow's card, the Coin spends 1 Rest to discard today's card and draw a replacement. Finish nine days with Dread below 6 and the yard sleeps.

The rule that makes it sing

The patron suits are fixed at setup — draw three cards, one per row from top to bottom, write each suit at its row's edge, and shuffle the cards back into the deck. Nine cards, four suits, three rows: one suit will always be wrong somewhere. The game is deciding which row to sacrifice before the deck decides for you, and then living with the Dread that follows.

Sim note: a thinking player wins 74.3% of the time. A player placing cards at random wins 6.5% — the gap between those two numbers is the game.

Played thousands of times by a computer

Before this post went up, the Cabinet was played more than fifteen thousand times by a computer — a simple bot for each game, playing the obvious strategy and nothing cleverer. Here is what it found, and what changed because of it.

Regent's Ledger could not be won. The original rules gave the computer a 0% survival rate — not a hard game, an impossible one. The total cost of paying every issue was about 150 Gold against 68 of income, and the total Loyalty damage from ignoring them was more than the four factions had between them. The first fixes — two issues a month instead of three, gentler ignores, 5 Gold of income — only got to 5%. What actually fixed it was starting Loyalty: 7 instead of 5 for every faction. That one number took survival from 5% to 38.3%. The treasury was never the problem. The factions' patience was.

The Auction could not be lost. The original rival purses were so small that they were empty by lot 10 of 20, and the computer swept the back half of the auction for a coin a lot — a 100% win rate with a margin of nearly a hundred points. The purses are now 55, 55, and 45, ties go to the player, and the win rate is 34.6%. The rivals can afford to want things now, which is the whole point of an auction.

The rest, in short: Lantern Keepers went from 5.6% survival to 35.0% with good play, once Wick became a real threat — it starts at 4 now, and without at least one repair it dies on night 12. The Debt went from 3.9% to 14.2% when the debt dropped to 80 and honest work started paying 7. The Gravekeeper went from 99.96% for a thinking player to 74.3% when the patron suits were fixed at setup instead of chosen by the first card placed. The Machine's campaign average rose from 5.0 to 5.79 out of 9 once the Spare Part was actually used. And the Cartographer's Lie needed nothing at all — 64.9%, first try.

One honest caveat, same as the other posts: the bot plays the obvious strategy and never gets tired, never gets attached to a card, never hides from a collector out of spite. A human will find these games harder than the numbers suggest — and easier in the ways that matter, because a human can see a trap coming and simply not walk into it.
