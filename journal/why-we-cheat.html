Why we cheat

In 2017, evolutionary psychologist David Buss published a paper with an uncomfortable thesis: affairs are not a malfunction of love. They are a feature of a mating system that was built, in part, for switching partners.

This is not an excuse. It is an explanation. And it starts with a strange fact about your brain: the circuitry for lust, the circuitry for romance, and the circuitry for attachment are three separate systems. They can run at the same time — aimed at different people.

Every claim below comes from peer-reviewed research. Sources are listed at the bottom.

1. Three systems, one brain

Anthropologist Helen Fisher proposed that mammals run three independent emotion systems for mating (Fisher, 1998). The critical part: they operate independently. Deep attachment to your partner, intense attraction to a coworker, raw lust for a stranger — all in the same week. That is not hypocrisy. That is neurochemistry. Tap each system.

2. Built to switch

In 2017, Buss and colleagues formalized the mate switching hypothesis (Buss et al., 2017): humans evolved not only to choose mates, but to replace them when a better option appears. An affair is often not about the current partner failing — it is about a new option clearing a threshold.

3. Who actually cheats

A national random sample (Atkins, Baucom & Jacobson, 2001) found the strongest predictors of infidelity. Answer honestly — four questions, then your attachment profile.

It is not the unhappy ones, necessarily. It is the ones with the doors open: prior infidelity is the single strongest predictor, and avoidant attachers — uncomfortable with closeness — stray more than anyone.

4. My take

Cheating is not proof that love is fake. It is proof that love is three systems sharing one skull — and they do not always vote the same way. Knowing which system is talking is the closest thing to a defense.
