How do you kill humanely?

On the evening of September 30, 2026, the state of Tennessee tried to execute Christa Pike — sentenced to death in 1996 for the murder of 19-year-old Colleen Slemmer, her fellow Job Corps student — with two doses of the sedative pentobarbital. She survived both. She complained of pain after the first dose, stayed conscious, and had to be rushed to a hospital by ambulance. She is believed to be the only person in American history to survive the administration of lethal-injection drugs.

This post is not about whether the death penalty should exist. It is about the narrow question the Pike case forced back into the news: by the available evidence, how do the methods actually compare — on speed, on consciousness, and on failure? The answer is messier than any of the names the methods were sold under.

Every figure below comes from a cited source — death-penalty research, autopsy studies, and contemporaneous reporting. Tap any source tag to see it.

1. Why "humane" is so hard to measure

There are three problems, and the first is final: the dead can't report back. Every claim about what an execution feels like is built from indirect evidence — autopsies, witness accounts, and the rare execution that failed.

The second problem is the paralytic. Three-drug lethal injection protocols pair a sedative with a drug that stops breathing and a drug that stops the heart. The anesthesia literature has a word for what can go wrong here: anesthesia awareness — waking up under drugs that hide your distress. A prisoner who is suffering but paralyzed looks, to witnesses, like a prisoner at peace.

The third problem is the lungs. In 2020, NPR obtained autopsy records from more than 300 lethal-injection executions across nine states. In the 216 reports with lung data, 84% showed pulmonary edema — the lungs filling with fluid. Normal lungs weigh about 400 to 450 grams each; the executed prisoners' averaged roughly 813 and 709. One reviewing physician said the fluid would create "a feeling of drowning, a feeling of suffocation — a feeling of panic, imminent doom." A federal court in Ohio, citing the same condition, compared it to waterboarding.

So the method designed to look the most peaceful — the prisoner quietly drifting off — is, by the autopsy evidence, the one where the most suffering may be happening invisibly. That is the paradox this post keeps returning to.

3. Ranked by the evidence

The Death Penalty Information Center's accounting of 8,776 American executions from 1890 to 2010 found an overall botched rate of 3.15% — and lethal injection, the method that looks the calmest, had the highest failure rate of any method: 7.2%. Since 2010, the lethal-injection rate has crept closer to 8%.

4. My take

Here's the paradox: the method that looks the most humane is the one with the worst numbers, and the method with the best numbers — the firing squad — is the one modern states keep coming back to precisely because it refuses to pretend. One of them is medicine's theater; the other is ballistics' honesty. Neither is what you could call clean.

The Pike case won't settle which method is humane. It did something stranger: a woman survived the procedure designed to be unfailable, and the state that tried to execute her had to save her life instead. That hour behind the curtain is the whole debate in miniature — we built a machine to make death look peaceful, and we can't even make it look certain.
