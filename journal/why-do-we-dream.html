Why do we dream?

You spend roughly six years of your life dreaming — a full feature film, every night, for a lifetime. So here's the honest scientific answer, up front: nobody knows for certain. There is no settled theory of why we dream. What we have is a machine we've mostly mapped, a shortlist of serious contenders with real evidence, and a few recent experiments that read like science fiction. 1. The machine

Dream science starts in 1953, when a graduate student wired up his young son's eyes and noticed something strange: the eyes moved — rapidly, in jerks — in cycles through the night, while the brain beneath them buzzed like it was awake. REM sleep. It rewrote the field: sleep wasn't one dim state, it was an architecture.

Sleep stagesN1 → N2 → N3 → back up → REM, then repeat
Cycle length≈ 90 minutes — 4 to 6 cycles a night
REM brainnearly as active as waking
REM bodyparalyzed — muscle atonia keeps you from acting out the dream
REM timinglengthens toward morning — the longest dreams are the ones your alarm eats

And here's why a dream feels realer than a daydream: in REM, the prefrontal cortex — your inner skeptic, the part that checks reality and keeps the story linear — goes quiet, while visual and emotional systems run hot. You're running the world's most vivid movie on a brain whose fact-checker called in sick. That's not a metaphor; it's the circuitry.

One more correction to a classic line: dreams are not a REM exclusive. Awakenings from non-REM sleep still produce mental reports about 43% of the time — less visual, more thought-like, but unmistakably there.

4. Why you forget

You don't fail to dream — you fail to keep the receipt. Dream recall is a memory problem, not a dream problem: the neurochemistry of REM (norepinephrine, the brain's "pay attention, this matters" signal, runs low) makes the memory trace fragile, and sleep's brain-wave patterns predict who will remember in the morning.

Dream researchers often repeat a figure: half of a dream fades within about five minutes of waking, most of the rest within ten. It's old, oft-quoted, and worth treating as folklore with a point — which is why the slider below is labeled illustrative.

The honest trick that works: keep a notebook by the bed and write immediately on waking. Attention consolidates. People who journal their dreams recall more of them — the brain starts filing the copies because someone is finally reading them.

5. Talking to a dreamer

In 1981, Stephen LaBerge did the experiment that made lucid dreaming scientifically respectable: he taught dreamers to move their eyes in a pre-agreed pattern — the moment they realized they were dreaming — and recorded the pattern on sleep-lab equipment while they were unambiguously in REM sleep. A signal from inside the dream, received in the lab.

Forty years later, four independent labs pulled off the next impossible thing: two-way communication with a sleeping person. Researchers asked lucid dreamers questions — "what's eight minus six?" — and the dreamers answered with agreed-upon eye sweeps, twice for "two." They did simple math, held working memory, distinguished stimuli. Twenty-nine correct answers across six individuals, all during polysomnography-verified REM sleep. The press called it talking to dreamers; the scientists called it "interactive dreaming." It is, right now, the closest thing to a telephone line into another person's mind.

The reality check, tonight

Lucid dreamers train this while awake. Tap each check to see what happens in a dream:

1. Count your fingers.
In a dream, hands go wrong — too many fingers, missing fingers, hands that melt. Look at your hands and count, right now. Make it a habit, and the habit follows you into sleep.
2. Read a line of text — look away — read it again.
In dreams, text changes. Words rearrange, signs dissolve. Read something, glance away, read it again. If it morphed, you're dreaming.
3. Push a finger into your other palm.
Awake, physics wins. Dreaming, your finger passes straight through. This is the classic: it works because dream-physics doesn't do solidity.

No guarantees — lucidity is rare and induction methods are inconsistent — but the checks are the standard field kit.

7. The practical bottom line

Whatever dreams are for — rehearsal, repair, filing, or byproduct — they happen inside a machine that only runs at full capacity when you sleep. Protect the night and you protect the theater:

The last third of the night is REM's country. Early nights are deep-sleep heavy; the long, vivid dreams live near morning. An alarm that cuts your last hour cuts your dreams.

Consistency beats catch-up. A regular schedule protects the architecture — the cycles need their ~90 minutes, in order.

Alcohol is a dream thief. It sedates you without giving you sleep's structure, and it suppresses REM specifically. You sleep; you don't dream.

My take: the question "why do we dream?" is still open, and that's not a failure — it's the most honest sentence in sleep science. The machine is mapped; the function is a live contest. So sleep first. The theater only shows its films to people who stay for the whole night.
