Are ghosts real?

EVERY CULTURE SAYS YES

Mesopotamia, Egypt, Rome, medieval England, your grandmother's kitchen — every civilization that ever existed has a ghost story. The details change. The dead showing up doesn't.

So the question isn't whether people see ghosts. People see ghosts all the time. The question is what they're actually seeing.

4. My take

I want it to be true more than I believe it is true. That's where most of us live, and it's worth saying out loud.

Because every famous case has a seam. Enfield's kids admitted they were playing around. Amityville's own lawyer said they cooked it up. The 1936 Brown Lady photo never got a sequel — and that was before everyone carried a camera in their pocket. Ghost reports used to come from whole villages. Then cameras got good, and the ghosts got worse.

But here's what I won't do: tell you the experience isn't real. The person who woke up paralyzed with a figure at the bedside saw something. The widow who heard her husband in the hallway heard something. The family in the "haunted" house with the bad furnace was genuinely terrified. The experiences are real. They're just not the dead.

My verdict: the dead aren't haunting us. The living are haunting themselves — with sleep cycles, sound waves, gas leaks, and grief. That's honestly more interesting than a ghost. It means the haunting is coming from inside the house, and the house is you.

One more thing: belief is a business. Ghost tours run thirty bucks a head, ghost-hunting TV needs a season two, and a haunted house sells for more than a plain one. Follow the money and you'll find a lot of the ghosts.

  3. Amityville — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amityville_haunting: DeFeo murders November 13, 1974; the Lutz family moved in December 1975 and left 28 days later; defense lawyer William Weber claimed in 1979 the story was invented "over many bottles of wine"; later owners reported no hauntings.
  4. Winchester Mystery House — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winchester_Mystery_House: San Jose, built 1886–1922 by Sarah Winchester; the "appease the spirits" legend largely postdates her death; renovations and piecemeal construction explain the oddities.
  5. Brown Lady of Raynham Hall — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_Lady_of_Raynham_Hall: photograph published in Country Life, December 16, 1936, by Captain Hubert C. Provand and Indre Shira; skeptics cite long exposure, double exposure, and light-smear explanations.
  6. EVP — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_voice_phenomenon: Friedrich Jürgenson, 1959; Konstantin Raudive, 1968; the leading skeptical account is auditory pareidolia.
  7. Hampton Court "ghost" CCTV — BBC News, December 2003: robed figure on security footage; palace officials suggested a visitor in costume and kept the footage for publicity.
  8. Vic Tandy — "The ghost in the machine" (1998, Journal of the Society for Psychical Research): ~19 Hz infrasound from an extractor fan produced cold shivers and a gray apparition in a Coventry lab; replicated in a controlled follow-up with Tony Lawrence.
  9. Carbon monoxide haunting — W. H. Wilmer (1921), American Journal of Ophthalmology: the "Mr. H" family heard footsteps and saw apparitions; traced to a faulty furnace; symptoms resolved after repair.
  10. W. D. Rees (1971), "The hallucinations of widowhood," British Medical Journal: 293 widowed subjects, 46.7% reported sensing the deceased spouse — a common, non-pathological experience.
  11. Persinger's "God helmet" and Granqvist et al. (2005), Neuroscience Letters: the replication found no magnetic-field effect; sensed-presence reports tracked suggestibility and personality instead. The debate is genuinely unsettled.
  12. Fox sisters — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fox_sisters: Hydesville, New York, 1848 rappings; Margaret Fox's 1888 public confession (toe-joint cracking), recanted in 1889.
