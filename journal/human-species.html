The human species: every member of our family tree

Published Wednesday, November 25, 2026

Today there is only one kind of human on Earth: us, Homo sapiens. That makes it easy to picture our story as a single ladder climbing from ape to modern person. The fossils say otherwise. For most of the past few hundred thousand years, several kinds of humans walked the planet at the same time, sometimes meeting and sometimes even having children together.

Our lineage split from that of chimpanzees and bonobos roughly 6 to 7 million years ago. Early hominins such as Sahelanthropus and Australopithecus (including “Lucy,” about 3.2 million years old) walked upright long before brains grew large. The first members of our genus, Homo, appeared around 2.8 million years ago. Below is each major human species in rough order of appearance: when it lived, where, and the key fossils that tell us it existed.

1. Homo habilis · “Handy man”

Time rangeabout 2.4–1.4 million years ago
RegionEast and southern Africa
Key fossilsOH 7 (type specimen, Olduvai Gorge, Tanzania), OH 24, KNM-ER 1813 (Koobi Fora, Kenya)

Homo habilis is the earliest widely accepted member of our genus. Its brain was only a little bigger than an australopith's, roughly 500 to 700 cubic centimetres, but its hands and teeth were already shifting toward a more human pattern. Louis Leakey and colleagues named it in 1964 because its bones turned up beside simple Oldowan stone tools: sharp flakes struck off river cobbles and used to cut meat and crack bones. Scientists still argue about whether habilis was really the toolmaker or just one of several hominins living near the tools, and some think the fossils grouped under this name belong to more than one species.

2. Homo erectus · The first traveller

Time rangeabout 1.9 million to roughly 110,000 years ago
RegionAfrica, western Asia (Dmanisi, Georgia), China and Indonesia
Key fossilsTurkana Boy (KNM-WT 15000, Kenya), Java Man (Trinil, Indonesia), Peking Man (Zhoukoudian, China), the Dmanisi skulls

With Homo erectus the human body plan arrives: long legs, shorter arms, a narrow waist and a frame built for walking long distances. Turkana Boy, a nearly complete skeleton of a youth about 1.5 million years old, shows how tall erectus could grow. Erectus made Acheulean hand axes, teardrop-shaped tools that stayed in use for over a million years, and it was the first hominin to spread far beyond Africa, reaching Georgia by about 1.8 million years ago and Java and China after that. It also lasted an astonishingly long time. The last known erectus fossils, from Ngandong in Java, date to roughly 117,000 to 108,000 years ago.

3. Homo heidelbergensis · The possible bridge

Time rangeabout 700,000–200,000 years ago
RegionAfrica and Europe (possibly Asia)
Key fossilsMauer jaw (Germany, type specimen), Kabwe 1 / Broken Hill skull (Zambia), Boxgrove (England)

Homo heidelbergensis had a brain nearly as large as ours, a big brow ridge and a heavy build. Sites linked to it show hunting of large animals and, in some places, use of fire. For years it was the favourite candidate for the last common ancestor of Neanderthals and modern humans, with European groups leading toward Neanderthals and African groups toward us. That neat picture is now debated, and some researchers prefer to split these fossils into several species, but heidelbergensis remains a handy label for the large-brained humans of the Middle Pleistocene.

4. Homo neanderthalensis · Neanderthals

Time rangeabout 400,000–40,000 years ago
RegionEurope and western and central Asia
Key fossilsFeldhofer Cave (Neander Valley, Germany), La Chapelle-aux-Saints (France), Shanidar (Iraq), Amud (Israel)

Neanderthals are the best known of our extinct cousins. Short, stocky and powerful, they were well suited to Ice Age Europe, and their brains were as large as ours. They made skilled stone tools, controlled fire, hunted big game, looked after injured group members and buried some of their dead. They disappear from the fossil record around 40,000 years ago, not long after modern humans spread into Europe. But they did not vanish completely: most people with ancestry from outside Africa carry about 1 to 2 percent Neanderthal DNA.

5. Denisovans · The DNA ghosts

Time rangeat least about 200,000 to roughly 50,000 years ago (possibly later)
RegionAsia: Siberia, the Tibetan Plateau and likely Southeast Asia
Key fossilsA finger bone and teeth from Denisova Cave (Russia), the Xiahe jaw (Tibetan Plateau, China)

Denisovans are the only human group discovered mainly through DNA. In 2010 geneticists sequenced the genome from a tiny finger bone found in Denisova Cave in Siberia and found it belonged to neither Neanderthals nor modern humans. Since then a few teeth and a jawbone from the Tibetan Plateau have been linked to them, but there is still no complete skeleton and no formal species name. Their legacy lives on in us: people in Melanesia carry several percent Denisovan DNA, and a Denisovan version of the EPAS1 gene helps many Tibetans live at high altitude.

6. Homo floresiensis · The “hobbit”

Time rangeabout 100,000–50,000 years ago (stone tools to about 50,000)
RegionFlores, Indonesia
Key fossilsLB1 from Liang Bua cave; older related fossils at Mata Menge (about 700,000 years)

When the skeleton known as LB1 was announced in 2004, it stunned scientists. This adult stood only about 1.1 metres tall and had a brain about the size of a chimpanzee's, yet stone tools were found in the same cave. Much older fossils at Mata Menge suggest small-bodied hominins lived on Flores for hundreds of thousands of years. One leading idea is island dwarfism: an earlier population, possibly descended from Homo erectus, gradually shrank on an island with limited food.

7. Homo luzonensis · The island puzzle

Time rangeabout 67,000–50,000 years ago
RegionLuzon, Philippines
Key fossilsTeeth, finger and toe bones and a femur from Callao Cave (species named in 2019)

Homo luzonensis was named in 2019 from a handful of bones found in Callao Cave on the island of Luzon. The fossils show a strange mix: small teeth that look fairly modern, but curved finger and toe bones similar to those of much older hominins, perhaps hinting at some climbing. Like floresiensis, it shows that human evolution on Southeast Asia's islands took unexpected paths. How its ancestors reached Luzon, which was never joined to the mainland by land, is still unknown.

8. Homo naledi · The cave mystery

Time rangeabout 335,000–236,000 years ago
RegionSouth Africa
Key fossilsMore than 1,500 bones from the Dinaledi and Lesedi chambers of the Rising Star cave system

Homo naledi was found deep inside the Rising Star cave system near Johannesburg, in chambers so hard to reach that the team recruited slim scientists able to squeeze through a gap about 18 centimetres wide. More than 1,500 bones from at least 15 individuals have been recovered. Naledi had a small brain, similar in size to early Homo, but human-like feet and hands, and it lived surprisingly recently, at the same time as early Homo sapiens in Africa. Whether naledi deliberately placed its dead in the cave is one of the most hotly debated questions in the field.

9. Homo sapiens · Us

Time rangeabout 300,000 years ago to today
RegionOriginated in Africa; now worldwide
Key fossilsJebel Irhoud (Morocco, about 300,000 years), Omo Kibish (Ethiopia, about 230,000+), Herto (Ethiopia, about 160,000)

Our own species appeared in Africa about 300,000 years ago, based on fossils from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco. Early sapiens had modern-looking faces but somewhat longer skulls than people today. A major dispersal out of Africa began roughly 70,000 to 50,000 years ago. Our ancestors reached Australia by at least 50,000 years ago, Europe about 45,000 years ago and the Americas by at least 15,000 to 20,000 years ago, interbreeding with Neanderthals and Denisovans along the way. Farming began about 12,000 years ago, and today more than 8 billion of us live on every continent.

The takeaway

Human evolution looks less like a ladder and more like a bushy family tree. Many branches grew side by side, and most of them ended. For a long stretch, Neanderthals, Denisovans, floresiensis, luzonensis, late erectus and our own ancestors all shared the planet. We are the last one standing, and we still carry pieces of our cousins in our DNA.

New discoveries keep reshaping the tree. Three of the nine species here (floresiensis, naledi and luzonensis) were described only in the last two decades, and the Denisovans were first identified from a single finger bone. The next cave dig or genome may add another branch.
