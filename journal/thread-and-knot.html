The Thread & The Knot

PEN · PAPER · CARDS

There is no board. There is a circle, thirteen named places around its rim, and six threads cast across it — and somewhere in the tangle, an answer you did not know you already had.

The Thread & The Knot is the quietest game in The Paper Table, and the strangest. It is a fortune-telling game for one person, one deck of cards, and a pen. You ask a question you genuinely do not know the answer to. You draw six cards and draw the threads they describe across a circle. Where the threads cross, knots form. Then you must cut one thread — and the knots on it dissolve, averted, while the knots that remain are what comes true. The verdict is not fate. It is a sentence you wrote, and then agreed to hear.

The Hollow Crown was a war. Suzerain was a conquest. This one is a conversation — with yourself, with a question, with a shape on a page that turns out to be saying something. It is the only game in the series where winning means being believed, and where the hardest move is not the cut you make, but the clear thread you choose not to cut.

The full rules are below, and the playable game page is linked at the bottom. Print it, ask it something real, and keep the ledger — over time it becomes a record not of predictions, but of questions.

✎ Pen▭ Paper🂠 One deck of cards◷ 20–30 min♟ 1 player — or 2, with the Oracle

1. A reading, worked through

Before the rules, a reading — so you can feel the game before you read it. A querent named Maren, a cartwright whose workshop burned in the spring, asks: "Will I find work before winter?" Six cards are drawn, six threads are cast, three knots form. Tap through the reading.

2. The question — and the Stake

Write your question at the top of the page, in ink. Phrase it as something you genuinely do not know the answer to. Good questions are specific enough to matter and open enough to be answered by a web:

  • Will I find work before winter?
  • Should I trust the offer from the stranger?
  • What is my brother hiding from me?
  • Will the harvest hold?

The Stake. Before casting, the querent writes down one suit they hope the verdict will be, and keeps it hidden until the cut. Cut the staked suit: +1 Reputation. Cut any other suit: −1 Reputation. If the staked suit has no thread in the web, the Stake is unmet: −1 Reputation. The Stake is a bet you make with yourself before you know what the web holds — it is the one part of the game where hope is written down first and judged later.

3. The loom — thirteen stations

Draw a large circle on the page, roughly the size of your open hand. Mark 13 dots around its rim, numbered clockwise from the top. These are the stations, and every thread will begin and end at one of them. They have names, because a thread that runs from The Field to The Door is a different story from one that runs from The Grave to The Crown.

StationNameStationName
1The Door8The Wood
2The Road9The Well
3The Hearth10The Chapel
4The Market11The Grave
5The Field12The Star
6The River13The Crown
7The Tower
Why thirteen?

Thirteen is the number of ranks in a suit, so every card can be placed without ambiguity. It is also, traditionally, an uncomfortable number — which suits a fortune-telling game.

4. Casting the web

Shuffle the deck. Draw six cards, one at a time. For each card:

  1. The rank gives one station. Ace = 1, 2–10 = that station, Jack = 11, Queen = 12, King = 13. (In the web, the King is the highest rank — this differs from most games.)
  2. The suit gives the other station: ♠ = 1, ♥ = 4, ♦ = 7, ♣ = 10.
  3. Draw a straight line — a thread — between those two stations. Write the card's suit and rank small beside the thread.
  4. If both stations are the same (for example, the Ace of Spades is rank 1 and suit 1), do not draw a line. Instead draw a small closed loop at that station. A loop is a special omen — see §6. A loop is never cut.

After all six threads are drawn, mark every point where two threads cross with a small circle. That circle is a knot. Do not count crossings at the rim (threads meeting at a shared station) — only interior crossings count. If three threads all cross at the same point, that is one knot involving three threads: read the dominant pair and note the third as an intensifier.

Coincident threads. If two cards draw the exact same thread (for example 4♥ and 4♠ both join stations 1 and 4), they count as a single thread. No knot forms between them. It happens in about seven of every hundred casts — rare enough to feel like an omen, common enough to need the rule.

A note on drawing

Threads should be straight. A web drawn carefully reads clearly; a web drawn sloppily lies to you, and the game has no way to know the difference. Take the minute. The loom is the whole game.

5. Reading the threads

Each thread is a current in the querent's life. Read all six, in the order you drew them. The suit is the nature of the current; the rank is its strength. Tap a current.

The thirteen strengths

RankStrength
2–4A faint current — barely felt, easily missed
5–7A steady current — present and reliable
8–10A strong current — impossible to ignore
J (11)A current that acts — a person or event that moves things
Q (12)A current that receives — an influence, a listener, a vessel
K (13)A current that rules — a dominant force, hard to resist
A (1)A current at a threshold — a beginning or an ending

Write one sentence for each thread. For example: "A steady current of gold — money is present but not abundant." Or: "A ruling current of iron — a conflict dominates the querent's life, and they cannot set it down." And read a thread by its endpoints too — Iron running from The Field to The Door is a different story from Iron running from The Grave to The Crown.

6. Reading the knots

Each knot is a moment where two currents meet. The two suits of the crossing threads give the omen. Read each knot in turn, and note its distance from the center of the loom: near the center, the omen is immediate and close at hand; near the rim, it is distant, approaching from far away.

PairOmen
♠ + ♠Iron on iron — a grinding conflict, strength against strength. Two forces that will not yield.
♥ + ♥Blood on blood — love that deepens, or love that wounds. Kinship tested.
♦ + ♦Gold on gold — fortune compounding, or greed that feeds itself.
♣ + ♣Root on root — knowledge that grows, or a hidden tangle that will not be pulled loose.
♠ + ♥Iron and blood — a love that must fight, or conflict inside the heart.
♠ + ♦Iron and gold — money at the point of a sword. Gain that comes at a cost.
♠ + ♣Iron and root — a lesson that hurts, or knowledge turned into a weapon.
♥ + ♦Blood and gold — love and money intertwined. A bond with a price.
♥ + ♣Blood and root — love that teaches, or the roots of family.
♦ + ♣Gold and root — wealth from knowledge, or knowledge that must be bought.

Loops

If a thread is a loop — a card whose rank station and suit station coincide — it has no knots of its own, but it colors every knot it touches. Read a loop as a closed circuit: ♠ a conflict with no exit; ♥ a love turned inward; ♦ a fortune that circles back; ♣ knowledge that feeds itself. A loop is never cut — it is already closed. If you draw two loops, the reading is doubly closed: the querent is caught in a cycle, and no single cut will free them.

7. The cutting

Now — and only now, after all threads and knots have been read — choose one thread to cut. Erase it. Every knot that lay on that thread dissolves: those moments are averted. The knots that remain are what comes true.

The card of the cut thread becomes the Verdict — the final answer to the question. Read it three ways:

The nature of the outcome — by suit

SuitOutcome
♠A hard truth. The outcome is difficult but strengthening. You will be glad of it later.
♥A favorable outcome in matters of the heart. Warmth, connection, repair.
♦Material gain or loss. Coin, work, exchange — the outcome is tangible.
♣A slow, hidden, enduring outcome. It will come quietly and last a long time.

The timing — by rank

RankWhen
AceNow. Immediately, or already underway.
2–5Within days.
6–10Within weeks.
J, Q, KWithin months.
The decision

You read the whole web before you cut. So by the time you choose, you know which threads carry which knots, and which verdicts they would produce. The art is in choosing the thread that gives the querent the reading they need — not necessarily the one they want.

8. Clarity — and Reputation

The number of knots on the cut thread determines how clearly the verdict lands. This is the game's only scoring — and it is not a win or a loss, but a quality of reading.

Knots on the cut threadClarityWhat it means
0–1ThinThe reading is faint. The querent doubts you, or forgets the verdict by morning. The verdict is half-true — it comes to pass, but not in the way you said.
2–3ClearThe reading lands. The querent believes it, remembers it, and it comes true as spoken. This is the reading a fortune teller hopes for.
4+TangledThe reading overwhelms. Too many currents meet on one thread; the querent is frightened or confused. The verdict is true but costly — it comes to pass, and something is lost in the passing.

Reputation — the campaign arc

Track Reputation in every reading, starting at 3. After each reading:

ReadingReputation
Clear reading, favorable verdict (♥ or ♦)+2
Clear reading, hard verdict (♠ or ♣)+1
Thin reading−1
Tangled reading−1 (but +1 if the verdict is favorable — the querent is impressed and shaken)
Cutting the staked suit+1
Cutting any other suit−1

At Reputation 0, your fortune teller is discredited — no one comes to the loom, and the campaign ends. At Reputation 10, they are renowned — the reading is sought by the powerful, and the final reading is a royal question: not "what will happen," but "what will you do about it." After that reading, the campaign ends. Reputation is the default arc of the game: every reading moves you one step toward disgrace or toward the palace.

9. The follow-up

After the reading, you may ask one follow-up question — a clarification, a consequence, a "but what about—"

Do not cast a new web. Read the remaining threads — the five you did not cut — as the answer. The follow-up is answered by what was spared, not by what was released. This is the oldest rule of the art: the answer to the second question is always in the first reading, and you simply did not see it.

10. Variants — and the Oracle

Once you have read a few webs straight, these are the ways to bend the game. The first of them turns a solitary art into a game for two.

VariantRule
Oracle and Querent — two playersOne player is the Oracle: they cast, read, and cut. The other is the Querent: they ask the question, secretly write a Stake (the suit they hope the verdict will be) before the cast, and judge the reading at the end — they say whether it landed Thin, Clear, or Tangled, by how true it feels. The Querent's judgment is the score, and the Oracle's Reputation moves by it. Swap roles for the next question.
Face-Down CastDraw all six cards face-down and draw the threads before revealing any suits. The geometry is fixed first; the meanings arrive afterward. This produces stranger, less legible webs — good for questions you are afraid of.
Double CastCast two webs for the same question. The thread whose suit appears in both webs is the true thread — cut that one, and the verdict is certain. If no suit repeats, the question is unanswerable tonight.
The Querent's ChoiceInstead of choosing the cut yourself, draw one card after the reading. The suit of that card determines which thread is cut (if two threads share that suit, cut the one with more knots). The querent, not the oracle, decides.
The Loop ReadingIf any card is a loop (rank station = suit station), the reading is closed. You may not cut a loop, and the verdict must be read from the loop's suit and rank instead of a cut thread. Closed readings are rare and always significant.
Three QuestionsAsk three questions before casting. Cast one web. Read the same web three times, once for each question — but you may cut only one thread in total, and the cut answers only one of the three. The other two are answered by the remaining threads, and are always Thin.
The SkepticA hard mode. After the reading, draw one more card. If it is black, the querent does not believe you; the verdict is true but will not be acted upon. If red, they believe. Face cards are ambiguous — the querent believes, but tells no one.
Keep the ledger

Keep a page for readings — the question, the six cards, the threads and knots, the thread cut, the verdict, the clarity, and whether it came true. Over time, the ledger becomes a record not of predictions but of questions — and that is the more interesting document.

11. Played 10,000 times by a computer

Before this post went up, the game was cast ten thousand times by a computer — not to play it, but to measure the shape of the web itself. How often does a good reading even exist? Here is what it found, and what changed because of it.

A Clear cut was rare. On the original rules — five threads — a Clear cut (a thread with two or three knots) was available in only 39.5% of casts. Nearly a third of all webs had no knots at all (30.9%), which meant most readings landed Thin before the oracle had even chosen. The geometry was not generous enough for the game the rules promised.

So the cast grew by one thread. Six threads instead of five. The extra thread crosses the others more often — mean knots per web rose from 1.44 to 2.15 — and the numbers moved:

Clear cut · 5 threads39.5%
Clear cut · 6 threads55.2%
Knotless · 5 threads30.9%
Knotless · 6 threads19.4%

The Stake got fairer too. With six threads, the staked suit has a Clear thread in 25.3% of casts (up from 16.6%), and the staked suit has no thread at all in only 18.3% (down from 25.2%). The Stake is still a gamble — that is the point of it — but it is no longer a gamble you lose before the cast is finished.

The worked example was wrong, and the computer proved it. The first draft's sample reading claimed four knots. Exact chord geometry says those five cards make zero knots — two of them share a station on the rim, and none of the others cross. Every cut in that example would have landed Thin. The sample in §1 was rebuilt from scratch on verified geometry: six cards, exactly three knots, all on the 5♣ thread. If you draw Maren's cast yourself, the knots will be where this post says they are.

One honest caveat: a Clear reading is still a coin flip, not a guarantee — 44.8% of casts have no Clear cut at all, even with six threads. Widening Clear to one-to-three knots would fix that, but it would water down what "Clear" means, and a one-knot thread is a faint reading wearing a clear name. The game keeps its meaning and accepts the odds. Besides, the sample reading above is the argument for it: the only Clear thread in Maren's web spoke of hidden knowledge, not work — and the right reading was the Thin one. A good oracle gives the reading the querent needs, not the clearest one on the loom.
